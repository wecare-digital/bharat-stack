# URL, host and home-fallback cleanup — public workspace links removed, legacy prefixes retired, return-path validator wired

The change removes the two public surfaces that handed customers a staff destination (the `ErrorBoundary` "Dashboard" button and three PWA manifest shortcuts), replaces the permissive post-sign-in `return` regex with a reject-by-default allowlist, restores the `www` → apex 301 that an earlier owner-instructed redirect purge deleted as collateral, narrows the `[retired public path ef531503]` → home 302 so the caller's query string is dropped, and lands a 100-row live probe harness plus pytest/vitest guards over all of it. It did **not** implement the plan it was written from: another session executed the owner's *"delete all url redirects now"* instruction first, taking the Amplify rule array from 146 to 8 and closing the original defect by deletion rather than by the planned `302 → /` conversion. The evidence document says so in those terms rather than retrofitting the plan onto the outcome.

Watch for: the change edits `src/pages/account/sign-in.tsx`, which this task's own plan marked DO NOT TOUCH — **confirmed**, and cleared by an orchestrator grant recorded outside the change in `docs/execution/change-authority-matrix.md`, then ratified by the owning workstream's own later commits keeping the wiring. The subdomain gap was closed by a sibling home-fallback workstream that created a CloudFront distribution, rewrote two hosted-zone CNAMEs and created then deleted an ACM certificate — **confirmed**, owner-instructed, attributed rather than claimed, and outside this task's writes. The plan's "all passthroughs precede every redirect" guarantee was **false in production** while the test that asserted it passed against a snapshot containing no redirect; corrected in place to the non-overlap property and re-asserted on the live shape.

**Verdict**: APPROVED

## High-level view

The hosting layer ends up with twelve Amplify custom rules: one host canonicalisation 301, three `[retired public path ef531503]` → `https://wecare.digital/?from=access` 302s, seven passthrough rewrites for `/get`, `/r`, `/api` and `/mcp`, and the `/<*>` → `/404.html` `404-200` catch-all last. The catch-all was never touched, which is the load-bearing decision — it is what keeps a mistyped URL at a 404 instead of an indexable home clone, and a non-404 status there would match unconditionally and shadow all ~123 exported pages.

Customers reach home by three different routes and they now agree on dropping untrusted input. A mistyped path gets the 404 document, which `router.replace`s to a literal `'/'` and carries a no-JS anchor; an unused single-label subdomain gets a 302 from a dedicated distribution that discards path and query; `[retired public path ef531503]/**` gets a 302 whose target carries its own `?from=access`, which is the documented mechanism for stopping Amplify forwarding the caller's query. That last one was a real defect the change found in its own plan and fixed rather than documented: `[retired public path ef531503]/?next=https://evil.example` used to redirect to `https://wecare.digital/?next=https://evil.example`.

Staff access is untouched. `_app.tsx` is unmodified across the whole task range, `/workspace/` still returns the Authenticator shell at 200 and is still `Disallow`ed, and the only `/workspace` destinations left in the tree are workspace-only chrome whose zero-external-importer assumption is itself asserted by test. Nothing was hidden in place of access control, and no authorization was removed.

The return-path validator is an allowlist of five destinations, every one checked against the filesystem by its own test so a member cannot be added without a page behind it. It rejects before matching: single leading slash only, no `\`, whitespace, control character, `%`, `?`, `#` or `:` anywhere, no `..` segment, no staff first segment — and the terminal comparison against a re-emitted allowlist member means an accepted value cannot smuggle a query or fragment through. It never decodes its input, which is what closes `%2f%2f` and `%252f%252f`.

The subdomain statement is exact and separates its dependencies. The certificate is not the gap: the shared SAN covers `wecare.digital` and `*.wecare.digital` to 2027-03-11. The single-label case is closed by a Route 53 wildcard pointed at a dedicated distribution, which deliberately sidesteps mutating the live domain association that serves the canonical home. The residual gap is second-label only and its cause is corrected from "no address" to "no certificate covers it" — `a.b.wecare.digital` resolves and dies at the handshake.

Evidence is measured rather than asserted. Thirteen of the forty-three matrix rows read *not measured before* instead of carrying a value, which is the honest shape for surfaces first probed in a later pass. Known defects that were not fixed — the unknown-API-path `302 → /contact/`, the `sw.js` push destination, four stale comments in unowned files — are pinned at their measured values or handed off by name, not quietly dropped.

### Requirements 2 and 5 pull against each other, and the shape resolves both

FEAT-002's `findings` opens with "WHY THIS FEATURE DID NOT DO WHAT IT SAYS" and names the four acceptance criteria that became unsatisfiable: emitting 45 rules at `302 → /` would have recreated redirects the owner had just retired, and the criterion that all 17 `RETIRED`/`FROZEN_EXTERNAL` sources still redirect was false because all 17 now 404 by instruction.

The brief's requirement 2 asks that the enumerated legacy prefixes "land on the canonical home". They do not 302 there — fourteen of fifteen terminate at 404 and arrive home through the 404 document's client-side replace. That is not a shortfall: requirement 5 explicitly demands the fallback stay a 404 rather than become an indexable 200 home clone, and the two requirements are only simultaneously satisfiable by exactly this shape. Measured independently:

```
[retired public path 84a04c24]/      404        [retired public path 44011e36]/   404        [retired public path 2c56bef4]/   404
/definitely-not-a-page/  404, body carries "page":"/404",
                         noindex, follow, canonical https://wecare.digital/
```

The no-JS path is a real link rather than a message about being redirected, so the one case the design cannot serve automatically still has an action.

### The ordering claim was wrong, and the test could not see it

`apply()` builds `desired_redirects() + [non-ours]`, so the host 301 and the three `[retired public path ef531503]` 302s sit at indexes 0–3, **ahead of all seven passthrough rewrites**. The plan's stated guarantee was the opposite. The assertion that existed, `test_every_passthrough_precedes_every_redirect`, read the committed 9-rule `after` snapshot — which contains no path redirect at all — so it passed while the guarantee did not hold in production.

The claim was restated to the property that is actually true (no redirect source equals or prefix-matches `/api`, `/get`, `/r/` or `/mcp`, in either direction), the snapshot test was renamed `test_in_the_committed_snapshot_every_passthrough_precedes_every_redirect` rather than deleted, and `test_no_sanctioned_redirect_can_shadow_a_passthrough_in_the_live_shape` now asserts against the array `apply()` writes. Non-overlap is the stronger property — a rule that cannot match an `/api` request cannot shadow it from any index. Requirement 4's "valid routes before the fallback" holds either way: the catch-all is last and a test pins it.

### The `[retired public path ef531503]` query-forwarding fix

Found by the change's own convergence pass, checking a plan claim nobody had probed. Design note D1 asserted that redirecting to a literal `/` dropped path, query and fragment. The path half was true; the query was appended. Measured before and after:

```
before   [retired public path ef531503]/?next=https://evil.example  302 -> https://wecare.digital/?next=https://evil.example
after    [retired public path ef531503]/?next=https://evil.example  302 -> https://wecare.digital/?from=access
         [retired public path ef531503]?a=b&c=d                     302 -> https://wecare.digital/?from=access
         [retired public path ef531503]/x/y?return=//evil           302 -> https://wecare.digital/?from=access
```

The mechanism is Amplify's documented behaviour that query parameters are not forwarded when the destination has its own, so the fix is to add one. `ACCESS_HOME_TARGET` carries that reasoning at the point of definition, including why `from=access` is inert — neither `index.tsx` nor `_app.tsx` references `location.search`, `URLSearchParams` or `router.query`, the status is 302 so no index equity moves, and it is a literal we emit rather than anything from the request.

The effect was inert before the fix, since the `Location` host is a fixed literal — but a requirement that untrusted query content be dropped is not satisfied by an attacker-chosen parameter arriving at a host that happens to ignore it. Three test sites plus matrix rows 93–95 pin the exact terminal URL, so reverting to the bare apex reinstates the defect loudly.

### The return-path validator

Adversarial reading of the rejection order found no bypass. The terminal comparison is against a re-emitted allowlist member, so even a value that survives every character check lands on `/cart/` unless it normalises to one of five literals. `/cart//` normalises to `/cart//` and is rejected. `/cart/.../` is rejected. A fullwidth solidus fails the first-character check. `%` is rejected outright rather than decoded, which is the only defensible choice — a validator that decodes has to pick how many times and the attacker picks the number.

The allowlist narrowing is the part that reads like judgement rather than process. `/checkout/` and `/account/` came verbatim from the plan and both measured 404 — `output: 'export'` emits a page only where a source file exists, and neither directory has an `index` — so wiring the validator with them present would have started routing signed-in customers to the missing-page document, a worse outcome than the redirect shape being closed. They were removed and `/blog/` added, with the reasoning that excluding a destination that resolves would *manufacture* the silent fallback the narrowing exists to avoid. `/terms/` is a real page and is still rejected, asserted explicitly, so membership stays a statement of intent rather than a consequence of existing. The test checks all five members against the filesystem, which makes re-adding either removed entry conditional on the page rather than on remembering the commit.

### The `src/pages/account/sign-in.tsx` crossing

The three-line edit — one import, one swap in `returnPathFromUrl()`, deletion of a comment claiming the replaced regex "can never be turned into an open redirect" — is a deviation from the brief's do-not-modify list, and it is the one place where the constraints are internally contradictory: requirement 6 asks for a reject-by-default return-path validator, and a validator with no caller leaves the permissive regex live. Both `//evil` and `/workspace/access` pass that regex, the second being the staff login.

What clears it is that the authority is recorded **outside** the change. `docs/execution/change-authority-matrix.md` cites the orchestrator's explicit grant and its written after-the-fact confirmation, the class, the target commit, the collision check at the time, the rollback, and an honest correction: the grant's stated basis was that the owning workstream had aborted, and that stopped being true the same day when it resumed. The correction strengthens the item rather than weakening it — `origin/stack`'s `sign-in.tsx` still imports `safeLocalReturnPath` and still returns it from `returnPathFromUrl()`, with `restoreSession()` and a `persistent` flag added *around* it. The path's own owner adopted the change rather than reverting it, which is better evidence than the absent-owner argument it replaces, and the superseded reading is kept rather than edited away.

The rollback note is the useful part: reverting reinstates both accepted inputs, so it is a deliberate security regression rather than a tidy-up, and the test asserts both that `sign-in.tsx` is a caller and that the literal `a-zA-Z0-9/_-` is absent from it — importing the validator while leaving the old regex beside it would otherwise satisfy a caller check and change nothing.

### Where the evidence is honest about its own gaps

`retired_url_probe.py` — a pre-existing guard the task did not write — holds `"www must 301 to the apex"`, so between the owner's removal and this change it was **failing**, exit 1. That is reported as independent confirmation that the host rule was genuinely missing, instead of the change vouching for its own repair.

The propagation reading is recorded as having looked like a failed change and not been one: ~25 minutes after the apply, `GET` on `https://www.wecare.digital/` returned 301 `Miss` while `HEAD` returned 200 `Hit` with `age: 1501`, from a one-year `s-maxage` on the apex whose cache key ignores the query string. It expired without an invalidation. The alternative — one probe, one conclusion — would have produced the wrong answer in either direction.

§9.2 records the tree moving mid-run: the same pytest command went from 14 passed to 2 failed with no edit, because another session rewrote the provisioner and applied it. Both failures were the task's own cross-file alarms firing, and both docstrings had pre-committed to "invert, do not delete" — which is the argument for pinning a known defect with a named test rather than a comment.

Two items I would have expected to find glossed and did not: the `shop.wecare.digital` row had its expectation **moved to the measured truth** rather than left red, with the reason stated (a gate that is permanently red for a reason nobody acts on stops being read) — defensible here because the behaviour change was deliberate and owner-instructed; and §9.6 flags that a concurrently-merged rewrite of the harness promoted `xout`/`www.xout` to hard gates and deleted ~50 lines of dated rationale, reporting it as an owner call for the workstream that made it rather than reverting it from here.
